# 01 Why Recovery Matters

Service recovery is the second half of service. The first half is doing it right. The second half is owning it well when we don't.

Every kitchen misses sometimes. The steak runs past temp. The buffet runs short. A special request gets dropped. What separates KitchFix from the operations the Clubs replaced us with is not that we never miss - it's that we recover differently.

> ANCHOR: A miss is not the failure. A miss without recovery is the failure.

## What this Playbook is for

This is not a reporting process. There is no log to fill out beyond the daily site log you already keep. What this Playbook does is teach the craft.

> NOTE: Safety incidents (allergen reactions, foodborne illness, injuries) are governed by SOP-002, not this Playbook. When in doubt whether something is quality or safety, default to SOP-002.

# 02 How Good Recovery Flows

Five moves a leader makes when something goes wrong. Not steps. Not deadlines. Moves that, taken together, describe what recovery looks like when it's done well.

## Move 1 - Acknowledge

First thing out of your mouth is acknowledgment. Not explanation, not defense. Acknowledgment names the miss, owns it as KitchFix's, and lands with the person who raised it - in twelve seconds.

<NonCanonical>
TRY THIS: "You're right, the steak came out overcooked. That's on us. Let me refire it for you right now."

NOT THIS: "There were some issues with the protein station today. We're looking into it."
</NonCanonical>

## Move 2 - Fix

Right action, right now. Refire the dish. Stand up the missing station. Get the right meal in front of the player who got the wrong one.

> ANCHOR: Don't promise what you can't deliver. A recovery that promises a make-good and then doesn't deliver is worse than the original miss.

## Move 3 - Tell Your People

The Site Leader keeps the RDO in the loop. The Sous Chef keeps the Site Leader in the loop. Information moves up so leadership has context when the same pattern surfaces a week later.

A useful check: "Would I want my manager to learn about this from me, or from someone else?" If "from someone else" is fine, it goes in the daily log. If "from me, before the end of the day," you call. If "from me, before the next service," you call now.

## Move 4 - Note It

The daily site log is the system of record. A line, two if needed: what happened, what you did, the root cause as you understood it. No separate Quality Recovery Log. No tier-classification.

<NonCanonical>
"Steak overcooked at lunch - vendor sent thicker cuts, Sous adjusted cook time after first miss" carries actual operational information. "Q2 incident, recovered" does not.
</NonCanonical>

## Move 5 - Carry the Learning Forward

A miss that was acknowledged, fixed, told, and noted but never actually changed anything is a miss that will recur. Sometimes the learning is a tweak to prep timing. Sometimes a note in the next pre-shift. Most recoveries don't need a system change.

# 03 What We Say. What We Don't Say.

The communication discipline that protects the relationship. Re-read this section at the start of every season.

## 3.1 What Good Recovery Communication Does

**Acknowledges specifically** - Name what went wrong in concrete operational language. Not "there were some issues."

<NonCanonical>
TRY THIS: "The steak ran past the temp we wanted. That's on us."

NOT THIS: "There were some issues with the protein station today."
</NonCanonical>

**Owns without explaining** - The recovery moment is not the moment for the why.

<NonCanonical>
TRY THIS: "That shouldn't have happened. Here's what we're doing right now."

NOT THIS: "The new vendor sent us a tougher cut, and we were short-staffed today, and the cook was still learning..."
</NonCanonical>

**States the recovery action** - Specific, in-the-moment, concrete.

<NonCanonical>
TRY THIS: "I'm refiring the steak now. I'll bring it to you personally in five."

NOT THIS: "We'll figure out what to do."
</NonCanonical>

**Closes the loop after the shift** - A recovery that ended cleanly in the moment still earns a brief end-of-shift note from the Site Leader to the Client lead.

## 3.2 What We Don't Say

- Don't blame the vendor - vendor problems are KitchFix problems to solve.
- Don't blame the team - "the new hire is still learning," "the Sous was off today" - staffing is a KitchFix issue.
- Don't narrate corporate - "corporate is making us...", "the new policy says we have to..." - internal disagreement is never shared with the Client.
- Don't defend at the moment of complaint - if the Client raised it, the spec was wrong, the execution was wrong, or the communication was wrong.

> ANCHOR: Per AGR-001 section 06: no PFS employee raises KitchFix-internal issues with the Client. Recovery communication is about the food and the service - not about KitchFix staffing, vendors, or internal disagreements.

## 3.3 Who Communicates

The Site Leader is the primary point of Client communication on quality issues. The Sous Chef and Hospitality Manager often communicate in real time during service. Ownership stays with the Site Leader, who follows up with the Client lead at end of shift.

# 04 How to Coach Recovery

Most leaders learn this skill the way most kitchen skills are learned - by being coached in real situations by someone who already does it well.

## 4.1 The Coaching Ladder

- Sous Chef to Line Cook - in the moment, at the station, focused on the fix and the next-time.
- Hospitality Manager to FOH Attendant - on language. The actual words. Roleplay if needed.
- Site Leader to Sous and HM - on the leader-level moves. Move 3 (telling your people) and Move 5 (carrying the learning forward) are what distinguish leader-level recovery from station-level.
- RDO to Site Leader - on patterns and Client trust over time.

## 4.2 In the Moment vs After the Service

Step in if the recovery is going off the rails - defensive language, escalating Client, wrong fix. Stand back if the recovery is just imperfect. The deeper coaching happens after, in the 1:1 or end-of-shift.

## 4.3 Skill Gap or Standard Gap?

When a leader misses recovery repeatedly, the diagnostic is whether they don't know how, don't care, or don't have the authority.

- Doesn't know how - skill gap. Coachable.
- Doesn't care - standard gap. The leader has the skill and is not deploying it. SOP-001 conversation, not coaching conversation.
- Doesn't have authority - system gap. Sometimes the right design; sometimes it's strangling recovery.

> ANCHOR: Most repeated misses are skill gaps, not character gaps. Default to coaching first.

# 05 How to Read Patterns

A single miss is service. A pattern is a system signal.

## 5.1 Four Common Patterns

- **Cook-skill pattern** - same cook, same kind of miss, even with coaching.
- **Vendor-or-supply pattern** - miss correlates with a specific vendor, ingredient, or delivery timing.
- **Service-flow pattern** - misses correlate with timing or sequence - not what was made, but when or how it got there.
- **Client-expectation drift** - nothing in execution actually changed but the Client is raising more concerns.

## 5.2 When a Pattern Becomes a Progressive Action Conversation

Most patterns get coached at the site level and never escalate. The judgment is qualitative.

- Same person at the center across multiple coaching cycles, and coaching has not produced change.
- Client has raised the broader pattern themselves, not just individual misses.
- Pattern is shifting trust at the leadership level.
- Pattern points to a leader who knows how to recover well and is not doing so consistently.

## 5.3 When a Pattern Triggers Other Systems

- Safety-adjacent root cause - cross-contact, undercooking, allergen-handling - stop. Re-evaluate against SOP-002.
- HR-grade conduct concern - directly to People Operations per AGR-001 section 06.
- Severe enough for formal action - SOP-004.
