*Who we are. Why we exist. What we believe. How we show up. The foundation every role in this Handbook rests on.*

# Why Culture Is First

KitchFix is a hospitality company. That is not a descriptor - it is the operating philosophy that everything in the company rests on.

Culture is our foundation because hospitality is not a department or a service standard. It is a way of seeing the people around you - the player walking through the line, the client who calls on a hard day, the cook who is three shifts in and still figuring it out. Our North Stars - the Mission, Vision, Values, and the Hospitality Promise - are not the culture. They are how the culture shows up in a specific role, on a specific day.

> ANCHOR: Without the culture, the role definitions in the Leadership OS Handbook are job descriptions. With it, they are how we build something worth belonging to.

The story below is where that culture comes from.

# Origin: Where It All Comes From

*What follows is the origin of KitchFix in the words of our founder and CEO, Josh Katt.*

## Developing the Craft

I started my culinary career working in a local pizza parlor in my hometown of Grand Haven, MI. I had no idea I'd build my life's work in hospitality, but I was drawn in by the people around me. At every step of my journey I was shaped and influenced by my coworkers, the organization, and the culture. I met many great friends along the way, created meaningful stories, and essentially grew up in culinary culture.

When I went off to junior college to become a production engineer in a music studio, an old coworker called and offered me a job in Grand Rapids. On my first day, my trainer whipped cream from scratch, scraping vanilla beans into the mix and whipping it with a fork to top a dessert, sliced into heirloom tomatoes, and plucked fresh herbs for garnish on a salad. My culinary perspective shifted. There were real skills to learn, there was a commitment to excellence, there were new ingredients to discover. I felt I was on the cutting edge of a world I was never shown or even heard about, and it was there that I fell in love with culinary arts. I wanted to become a chef.

At that time I never really thought about hospitality. I thought about becoming the best chef I could be, I thought about getting in early to create new dishes, I thought about impressing those around me, I thought about making beautiful food, and I thought about doing all of it efficiently. The art and craft of culinary arts is what I first fell in love with, and I wanted to master it.

I switched my major to culinary arts and worked while I attended school. One of the most accomplished chefs in my program was vegan. Diagnosed with diabetes after years of a regular diet, he had committed to eliminating animal products and built his entire philosophy around clean, purposeful cooking. He showed me techniques that would later become the foundation of KitchFix: nutritional yeast for umami, cashew cream, tamari and coconut aminos. He shared a book with us, Diet for a New America by John Robbins, which makes the case that factory farming is destroying human health and the planet, and that clean, humanely raised, whole food is both a moral and health imperative. That book changed how I saw ingredients. Food wasn't just fuel. Food was medicine.

My mentor chef, who was from my hometown, gifted me my first cookbook, Charlie Trotter's. My culinary instructor showed us restaurant websites every Friday, walking us through what different chefs were doing, their origin stories, what made them remarkable. He showed us Le Lan in Chicago, a French-Vietnamese restaurant with chef-partners from both worlds and a chef de cuisine from the French Laundry, and I remember hearing the buzz about Alinea, a new spot at the time pushing the boundaries of what food was. I wanted to keep chasing the cutting edge, and when an old restaurant friend moved to Chicago, I followed.

I staged at Le Lan and knew it was the place for me. French top stoves, a rational oven, a hard-to-please chef, doing things with food I hadn't seen before. I applied, got the call back in Grand Rapids, and was offered $80 a day, no overtime, sick pay, or insurance. I countered. We settled on $85. It was my first real disillusionment with the world I had come to love. I didn't last long. The 16-hour days were grueling, the culture was unforgiving, my coworkers imitated that top-down, dictatorial leadership style, and I knew I wanted something different. I handed in my apron and decided to explore other parts of the city's culinary scene.

Over the next few years I worked through Chicago's culinary scene: a neighborhood deli in Bucktown where I became kitchen manager and got back to the fundamentals, a large catering company cooking for thousands at the Field Museum and Adler Planetarium where the food was sometimes mediocre but the culture was warm and the people were genuinely happy, and eventually an iconic lunch spot where I became sous chef and rebuilt my culinary confidence under a team of mostly female chefs who had incredible backgrounds and helped me find my footing again.

But something was pulling me in a different direction. The city I had discovered called out to me. There was so much disparity, homelessness, and racial inequality, and I wanted to see what I could do. I enrolled in bible college to study urban studies. I hung up my clogs and pursued other paths.

During bible college I picked up serving jobs, my first, and became a culinary instructor for Common Threads, a non-profit that taught inner-city kids how to cook, exposing them to new cultures and expanding their horizons. It was the most fulfilling work I'd done. I loved the kids, I loved showing them my love for culture and cuisine, and eventually I earned a role as a program lead, had a desk job during the day and taught classes in the afternoon, while picking up catering gigs on the side and learning about compassion at school.

Then a board member at Common Threads reached out. She was recovering from cancer and wanted to eat the anti-inflammatory diet. She asked me to cook for her weekly.

That work brought together everything I cared about: sourcing quality ingredients, focusing on skills and techniques to build flavor using spices and herbs, crafting creative recipes without the use of gluten or dairy, all while making food genuinely delicious. My client loved the service and told her friends, I picked up more and more business, a website and blog I built started bringing in more and more clients, and I had built a stable personal chef practice. But I knew I needed to grow it further, and without my own space I wouldn't be able to get my prices down to reach the masses or scale. It was time to get a kitchen.

I noticed the church I attended had an underused kitchen and inquired about renting it. My pitch was that in addition to running my business, I would seek to employ folks from second-chance programs, people rebuilding their lives after recovering from addiction or homelessness. My first two hires had zero culinary experience. Perfect. The business took off, I could offer lower prices, and I had the ability to craft the food more efficiently. And when the rent bill came due it lit a fire. I needed to scale this thing bigger and quicker than I thought.

This is how KitchFix was born: out of a desire to make high quality food that did more than just feed people but fuel them properly and heal them, to create a different type of culinary culture, one that gave back, one that brought others along, and one that still had high standards, was on the cutting edge, and offered more than just a job, but an opportunity to build a career for those I worked with, and myself.

## The Early Days of KitchFix

We launched in 2012 as a healthy meal delivery company: dairy-, soy-, corn-, and gluten-free, pasture-raised meats, eco-friendly packaging, working with local farmers. Business boomed early, and I was forced into a new type of leadership while the kitchen executed. Along the way more and more inquiries came in. People wanted our snacks in grocery stores, teams wanted our food in their clubhouses, and we entertained it all. I raised money to expand the business and got in front of our growth needs by signing a 10-year lease for a kitchen build-out on the near west side of Chicago.

With more exposure, with investors, and with more and more overhead, we needed to grow revenue in whatever way we could. Times were tough. We zigged, we zagged, we all had long days and long sleepless nights, all while I was personally being pulled from the kitchen to the desk and from building culture and cuisine to building Excel workbooks and pitch decks. I was taking the profits from one side of the business to fund another, I was asking employees to shift their focus each week, I was changing the plan and vision annually to accommodate our latest projections. I was burning out fast, and burning out cash faster.

"We're going to run out of money in 6 weeks," I told my advisory board. "If we don't raise more capital, we're done." The response: "Well, I hope you can do it." They were off the phone, and at about 8 years into the business, I knew I was going to have to make tough choices yet again. Choices that would impact people, and hopefully save the business.

This forced me to become laser focused on our financials, to look at where all the money was going, to dig deep into the profits and losses of each product we made. Over the next 3 years I committed to running profitably, to not raising more capital, and to turning the business into something I loved again. We had built so many different things and we had people everywhere, products everywhere, meetings everywhere, and I just couldn't do it anymore. Yet what we were doing at Wrigley Field for the Cubs and in Dunedin for the Toronto Blue Jays seemed like a bright spot. That business had consistent income, it had happy clients, and it brought back my passion for culinary and what I would eventually see as hospitality. Being able to serve fresh food that met the client's needs and to see their reaction to our culinary philosophy live in the moment was meaningful. It brought fulfillment from a hospitality perspective and a culinary perspective. I had found my roots again, combining a solid business model with the opportunity to serve clients' needs where they were, build a team while joining theirs. KitchFix Performance Food Service was born in this.

I should say plainly: I didn't do any of this alone. The leaders, cooks, and partners who chose to build this with me are as much a part of this story as I am, especially in the later years. From those committed to finding every dollar, to those who made operations work on a shoestring budget and the sweat of their brow, to the people who cared deeply about this work and lived this handbook before it was written, KitchFix became what it is because of all of them.

## The Standard

I didn't have a word for what I'd been chasing this entire time until a few years ago.

That word is hospitality.

In the culinary world, you're often treated as the lowest on the totem pole. There is something about that that I like. It's an easy way to be of service, because in some ways you have no choice, nobody is looking out for you from above, and it forces you and your team to look out for each other, to build a culture and team to execute the job of service. A culture committed to each other to serve is powerful, and a core tenet of what we do at KitchFix.

Hospitality is finding the joy in service despite our positioning toward our client. Through our work, we can bring joy to our end user, and tapping into that pursuit is what brought all of my worlds together: the commitment to striving to be the best in a healthy way together with a team, to love what we cook and bring our authentic selves into each dish, to pursue our clients even when they don't want to give us the time of day, to seek ways to meet their needs, to be a well-rounded company that is sustainable and ethical, to commit to all parts of the craft. This is hospitality at KitchFix.

In the early days this showed up in high quality customer service via email to clients we couldn't see, always giving them the benefit of the doubt. It was becoming one of the first hospitality companies in Chicago to offer paid sick time for hourly employees even before it was mandated. It's providing generous severance at times, a fair wage, a commitment to coaching and second chances, a commitment to a good culture even though it's not perfect and hard decisions are sometimes made. It's a commitment to getting better together.

I want to take what we've built and bring more people into our world, both employees and clients. This Handbook is about outlining, in as much detail as we can, how to do what we've always been known for, what it will take from all of you to grow our business, how we as a company will operate, what we will expect from you, and our commitment to helping us all get there. It's our commitment to becoming remarkable and growing our passion for hospitality in an unstoppable way.

# Hospitality: What We Mean

Hospitality is the practice of anticipating the needs of the people around you and acting on them with genuine generosity, especially for those who need it most. Some days it's instinct. Some days it's a choice. We practice it either way.

The word gets used a lot of ways. Most of them are wrong.

Performed hospitality is a script: a smile at the door, the right phrasing, the practiced warmth that disappears the moment the guest turns their back. Scripted hospitality is a checklist dressed up as caring. Transactional hospitality is the clearest version of the problem: you showed up, you served the food, they ate, everyone goes home. Something happened. Nothing landed.

Genuine hospitality is something different. It is the decision to see the person in front of you - not the function they serve, not the request they're making - the actual person. The player who's had three bad at-bats and is walking through the line not making eye contact. The client who calls on a Friday with a problem that isn't really about the food. The cook who is learning something for the first time and needs someone to stay with them a minute longer.

> ANCHOR: That is the operative word: genuine. And it cannot be performed. It has to be inherent.

## Service Is the Table. Hospitality Is Why They Come Back.

Service is anticipation: having the food ready, the line clear, the labels accurate, the operation set up so that no one has to want for anything. Service is the foundation. Without it, there is no hospitality to offer.

But once service is right, once the guest needs for nothing, something else becomes possible. The guest can be present. And so can you. That is the moment hospitality happens. It is not a department. It is not a training module. It is what emerges when a person genuinely decides to see another person and act on it.

## What This Looks Like at KitchFix

It is learning a player's name and saying it. It is noticing when someone is having a hard week and asking a real question. It is the cook who finishes a dish and thinks about the person who's going to eat it. It is the site leader who reads the room in a client conversation and responds to what is actually being said, not just what was asked. It is the hospitality manager who finds the small thing - a player's dietary request, a birthday, a welcome-back after a long road trip - and makes it a moment.

It is not grand gestures. It is consistent attention.

## Why This Matters Here

KitchFix is in a business that puts us in the middle of people's work lives, every single day. Players eat with us more often than they eat with their families during the season. We are not background. We are part of the rhythm. That is an unusual privilege, and an unusual responsibility.

The standard we hold ourselves to - Best Food, Best Service, Best Hospitality - is not a marketing line. It is a daily commitment to keep showing up with the genuine thing. When we do that right, people notice. Not always consciously, but in the way they feel when they walk through our line. Safe. Seen. Glad they're here.

That is the standard. Your job is to build the team and the environment where it happens every day.

# Our North Stars

*Mission, Vision, Values, and the Hospitality Promise. The four fixed points every leader at KitchFix navigates by - the things that don't change when everything else does.*

## Mission: Why We Exist

**We're here to do more than just feed people.**

Genuine is the operative word. Performed hospitality, scripted hospitality, transactional hospitality - none of those are what we do. Players, coaches, dietitians, Athletic Training, Strength and Conditioning, front-office staff, and our own teams should all be able to feel the difference between a service operation and a hospitality operation. That is the bar.

## Vision: Where We Are Going

**Be the vital partner.**

> PROMISE: <Fact id="vision_statement" />

Vital partner is a high bar. It is the standard you hold yourself to before anyone holds it for you. Every meal period, every conversation, every walk through the dining room is a chance to be irreplaceable to our client and those we serve.

## Values: What We Believe

*We didn't write these values - we found them. When we started to focus on what was working at KitchFix, what was left told us who we were. We look for these values in who we hire and who we promote.*

- **Humility** - We are a company of humble individuals who support each other to get better every day. Senior people stay teachable. Junior people are heard. Egos do not run our kitchens. It's why the best people here stay teachable. Today's standard is not tomorrow's ceiling. When things break - and they will - humility is what shows up first. Not defensiveness, not explanation. The humble move is to fix it, own it, and understand it. In that order.
- **Intentionality** - We grow with intention, passion, and creativity. Decisions are made on purpose. Menus are designed, not assembled. Hires are deliberate. Standards are written down so that every operator can hold them.
- **Sustainability** - We maintain sustainable growth and profit. Service that burns out the team is not sustainable. Pricing that erodes margin is not sustainable. We build operations that can run season after season without breaking the people in them. It's how we survived the hard years and how we build for the next ones.
- **Equity** - We are striving to be more giving, equitable, and inclusive, internally and externally. Our hourly teams are treated with the same respect as our executive team. Bilingual leadership matters. Every voice in the operation deserves a hearing. It's why our first two hires had zero culinary experience. It's why we offered paid sick time before the law required it. It's why we pay 1.5x when we ask our hourly teams to work on holidays.

## The Hospitality Promise: Best Food, Best Service, Best Hospitality

> PROMISE: <Fact id="brand_promise" />

Three words. The brand promise the client is buying, the standard our people deliver, and the daily reminder of why this work matters.

### Best Food

Chef-crafted, nutrient-dense, performance-aligned. The four culinary principles - <Fact id="culinary_principles" /> - are how we hold this standard every day. We cook food worth talking about, built for the team we serve. We cook it the way it was meant to be cooked. Every ingredient earns its place, and every dish is one our chefs would proudly eat themselves. Food is not just fuel here. It's medicine, craft, and care - the way it has been since the beginning. ### Best Service

Service is anticipation: the food ready, the line clear, the labels accurate, the operation built so no one has to want for anything. We deliver what others promise. We anticipate before we react. When service is right, something else becomes possible: the guest can be present. And so can we.

### Best Hospitality

Genuine hospitality - not performed, not scripted, not transactional. The practice of anticipating the needs of the people around you and acting on them with genuine generosity. It is learning a player's name and saying it. Noticing when someone is having a hard day and asking a real question. Making the small thing - a dietary request, a birthday, a welcome-back after a long road trip - a moment.

# Our Promise to You

**KitchFix is a great place to work.**

The work is interesting - culinary, creative, always evolving, at the intersection of food and performance. The culture is genuine. You'll be challenged, you'll grow, and you'll be part of a team that actually cares about the people on it. We built this place to be what we always wanted to work in, and we protect that standard every day.

**We give you what you need to do the job.**

The tools, the clarity, the training. You shouldn't have to guess at the standard or figure it out alone. That's on us.

**This is a job, not your whole life.**

We build operations that are sustainable for the people running them. Burnout is not a business model. We expect your best at work, and we expect you to have a life outside it.

**We invest in your growth.**

Coaching, development, and - when we execute well together - real opportunities to grow in pay, responsibility, and role. What we build here is meant to create a bigger company to belong to.

Our commitment is to put every leader in a position to succeed - the coaching, the clarity, and the culture to back it up - as they hold our values and bring their best to the work. That is how we become remarkable.

# The Vital Partner Standard

*What every leader is accountable to make true for every client we serve.*

**We hold the standard - and then exceed it.**

KitchFix owns the bar. We don't look to the client to set it, remind us of it, or hold us to it. The Service Level Agreement (SLA) defines what we're contracted to deliver. Our standard goes further - the Hospitality Promise is the floor, not the ceiling. We show up to exceed it, not meet it.

**We run toward problems, not away from them.**

When something breaks, the client hears it from us first, not the other way around. Proactive, honest, solutions-forward. We earn the partnership by being the kind of operator they never have to chase.

**We integrate, not just show up.**

We learn the team, the staff, the season, the facility. We're not a vendor cycling in and out. We become part of the operation, and that's what makes us irreplaceable.
