## 4. THE BIG OPERATIONAL FINDING — the meal→snack mix shift (revenue erosion)
This is the theme the P&L hinted at and the OneSheeter makes explicit. Across nearly every account, **actual meal counts came in UNDER budget while snacks/continental came in OVER** — and since snacks bill at far lower rates, revenue eroded even where headcount held:

- **TXR-AZ**: MLB meals −1,157 vs est (−$32,257 SF-adj), MiLB meals −1,469 (−$20,478). BUT Hot Snack +1,000 (+$10,660), Regular Snack +1,555 (+$8,926). Net revenue −$33,149. KF note: *"substitution of meals for hot snacks which come at a deficit in revenue."*
- **CIN**: MiLB meals −4,365 (−$54,170); Continental +1,085 (+$6,640); Rehab −1,875 (−$23,269). KF note: *"switched to a continental/snack sooner… the shift to cont plus is double the projected, while taking away the buffet meals (less rev/count)."* Down 5K meals YTD.
- **TBJ**: MiLB meals −3,112 (−$34,605); MLB +1,120 (+$24,920) partially offsetting.
- **TBR**: mixed — MiLB Lunch strongly over (+1,670, +$35,321) and big à-la-carte protein sales (+$55K combined), but MiLB Breakfast under. Net actually POSITIVE (+$134,712) — the outlier that held.

**Why this matters for the SC and the bill export**: the calendar has to represent snack/continental services as *distinct billable line items at their own rates*, not fold them into "meals." If the SC models a service day as "a meal" when operationally it became "a hot snack," the projected bill overstates revenue. This is an argument FOR the granular per-service pricing the audit already found in PG (which stores snacks/continental separately) — the mix shift is exactly why that granularity is load-bearing. It's also a **projection-accuracy** point more than a billing-correctness one: the rates are right; the *count mix* is what drifts.
